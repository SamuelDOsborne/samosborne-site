From the Other Side of Care

He has been the frightened person in the room.

In 2008, a piece of metal tore through Sam’s left eye and pierced his retina. He nearly lost his sight. For a while he was the frightened patient on the other side of care, the person your staff meet every day. His eyesight recovered. His relationship with perception did not. What he teaches now came out of that question: how the way we see a moment decides how we react to it, on both sides of the bed.

Before You Book

The questions planners
actually ask.

Is this a resilience or wellness talk? ▾

No. It’s a practical, step-by-step communication and composure skill your people can use with the next upset patient or family. If they can’t use it on the next shift, it didn’t work.

Is this therapy or mental-health treatment? ▾

No. The program is educational and developmental. It doesn’t diagnose or treat any condition, and it isn’t a substitute for your employee assistance program or for licensed care.

Does it replace our de-escalation training? ▾

No, it complements it. De-escalation training teaches what to do when a situation escalates. This works on the moment before that: how your people see the person in front of them, so they stay steady enough to use the protocol.

Can attendees earn CE credit? ▾

The program isn’t pre-approved for any credit. Many hospitals and associations are approved providers themselves and can offer credit for sessions they host. I’ll supply everything the application needs: outline, learning objectives, timed agenda and bio.

What if nobody volunteers? ▾

The ask is much smaller than it sounds, which is why hands go up. Nobody is asked to tell a story or disclose anything private, and no patient information is ever involved. They name a person and one observable behavior, and Sam does the rest through questions. A volunteer is also agreed with the host in advance as a backstop, so the segment runs either way.

Is this safe for a professional audience? ▾

Yes, and that is the whole craft of it. The method deliberately strips the drama out and works on observable behavior instead, so the volunteer is never exposed. As one attendee put it: he takes someone through a difficult emotional process while still giving them the safe space to get there.
